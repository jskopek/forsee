# Tiny purpose-built models make offline AAC real

Everything the project wants can run on-device and offline today in some form, but the three capabilities sit at very different levels of maturity, and the best version of each comes from small purpose-built models, not from large general ones. Word prediction is the most ready: a 25-million-parameter model trained for typing fits in about 28 MB, answers a full keystroke query in roughly 25 ms on a laptop CPU, and saves about 9 to 11 points more keystrokes than an n-gram, so the v1 plan's premise that on-device language models are too slow to be a default holds only for general chat models. Speech-to-text of conversation partners is ready on all four platforms at a planning accuracy of 10 to 20% word error rate in real rooms, and "who spoke" works best as matching against a few enrolled household voices, since open live diarisation still mislabels 21 to 55% of speech time. Human-sounding speech in the user's own voice is ready on Macs, recent iPads and most laptops through zero-shot cloning from a few seconds of clean historical audio, is marginal on mid-range Android, and has only weak emotional control at that size. Context-aware sentence replies are feasible in compute terms on Macs and flagship phones, but the only rigorous eye-gaze evidence shows a 29 to 60% speed gain for two users, half of what simulation predicts, and personal memory matters more than model size. A capable coding agent changes the picture most for prediction, where training a custom keyboard model takes under an hour on one laptop GPU and every building block is open. Many primary sources were unreachable during research, so each claim below carries a tag showing how it was checked, and the pivotal prediction numbers come from one open project's self-reported benchmarks.

## How to read the evidence tags

Three kinds of statement appear in this report and they are not equally reliable. **[read]** means a researcher opened the primary source in the research session, which in practice meant GitHub READMEs, repository documents and a few Apple and Android developer pages. **[extract]** means the claim comes from a search-engine extract of the cited page, because arxiv.org, huggingface.co, most vendor sites and most paper hosts could not be opened; treat every [extract] number as reported but not checked against the original table. **[weak]** marks a secondary blog, aggregator, vendor claim or single anecdote. **[estimate]** marks the researchers' own arithmetic or engineering judgement, which no source states.

Two consequences matter throughout. First, almost every model-weight licence lives on a Hugging Face model card that nobody could open, so weight licences are [extract] or unverified unless the project's own README states them; every licence must be re-read before anything ships. Second, there are almost no clean measurements on two of the four target device classes: ordinary Windows laptops without a discrete GPU and mid-range Android tablets. Most statements about those two classes are [estimate].

## A 25M keyboard model beats models forty times larger

### What works today

The strongest finding of the deep dive is that word prediction does not need a large model. Typeling, an Apache-2.0 project published in November 2026, trained a **25.4M-parameter** model that occupies **27.55 MB** at 8-bit and runs at **2,800 tokens per second** for single-token decode on a laptop CPU with four threads [read] ([Typeling model plan](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling/blob/master/docs/model-plan.md)). A complete keystroke query, meaning constrained beam search, typo repair and n-gram fusion in unoptimised PyTorch, averaged **23 to 25 ms** on chat text with a 95th percentile of 39 to 45 ms [read] ([Typeling model plan](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling/blob/master/docs/model-plan.md)). On a sealed chat test set it saved **58.0%** of keystrokes with a three-candidate bar, against **48.8%** for a 5 MB trigram and 50.6% for an unpruned 23 MB n-gram [read] ([Typeling README](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling)). When every word of three or more letters was mistyped, the n-gram fell from 49.6% to 40.5% while the neural model fell only from 59.4% to 55.7% [read] ([Typeling README](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling)). That robustness to noisy input is directly relevant to gaze typing, where the wrong key is hit often.

Three other sources point to the same size class. Apple's keyboard transformer, introduced in iOS 17 and run on every key press, was estimated by an outside reverse-engineer at about 34M parameters with a 15,000-token vocabulary [extract; an outside estimate, not an Apple disclosure] ([Simon Willison summary](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Jun/3/transformer-powered-predictive-text/)). FUTO Keyboard ships a roughly 36M-parameter Llama-architecture model with a 15,008-token vocabulary on llama.cpp [read, from a community reverse-engineering document, not FUTO's own docs] ([futo-basque research notes](https://github.com/itzune/futo-basque/blob/main/RESEARCH.md)). The Lexo keyboard author found that moving from a 0.5B to a 1.5B model raised next-word top-1 accuracy only from **19.0% to 20.1%** at about 2.4 times the cost, and concluded the task is capped by how predictable short informal text is, not by model size [extract] ([Lexo write-up](https://lexokeyboard.app/writeups/on-device-llm-keyboard/)).

Domain training matters more than size. The same 25M base scored 33.5% chat keystroke savings before domain training and 43.1% after continued pretraining on about 150M tokens of chat and writing text [read] ([Typeling](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling)). For AAC specifically, Gaines and Vertanen's best domain-adapted model gained **5.5 points** of absolute keystroke savings over a strong AAC n-gram in simulation, at 55 to 120 ms per character prediction against 0.1 ms for the n-gram [extract] ([arXiv 2501.10582](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.10582)). The two results use different test sets and cannot be added together. A fair expectation is 5 to 11 points of keystroke savings over an n-gram [estimate].

The n-gram does not disappear. Typeling's pruned 5 MB trigram answers in 41 to 115 microseconds in pure Python [read] ([Typeling README](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling)), mixing it into the neural result at weight 0.1 adds 0.2 to 0.3 points of keystroke savings, and a count-based user cache of the person's own words is the personalisation method with evidence behind it [read] ([Typeling model plan](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling/blob/master/docs/model-plan.md)). On-device LoRA personalisation exists in FUTO Keyboard but its own wiki describes it as disabled by default and possibly ineffective [extract] ([FUTO wiki](https://gitlab.futo.org/keyboard/keyboard-wiki/-/wikis/FUTO-Keyboard/diff?version_id=1dfd9e50b673c2c695fc844a4fd1e6f16b71000d)).

### How to get letters and words out of a subword model

The decoding method is settled and open. The Vertanen group's algorithm, documented in BciPy and implemented in the TextSlinger library, removes the tokens of the partly typed word from the context, runs a beam search that keeps only tokens consistent with the typed letters, and sums the probability of finished hypotheses by the character that follows [read] ([BciPy language README](https://github.com/CAMBI-tech/BciPy/blob/main/bcipy/language/README.md), [TextSlinger](https://github.com/kdv123/textpredict)). The paper reports this search beats a character classification head, a byte-level model and an n-gram [extract] ([arXiv 2501.10582](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10582)). One search per keystroke therefore yields three outputs the v1 plan already wants: next-letter probabilities for adaptive dwell, word completions and next-word candidates [estimate, a design inference from the algorithm].

FUTO adds a second useful idea. It feeds each typed key as a dedicated keystroke token and can represent an uncertain key press as a weighted mixture of key embeddings [read, community document] ([futo-basque research notes](https://github.com/itzune/futo-basque/blob/main/RESEARCH.md)). For gaze input this means a fixation that lands between two keys can be passed to the model as a probability over keys. One hobby gaze-typing project found that free character-level beam search with a 4-gram was too weak at about one key of gaze error and replaced it with a lexicon decoder [read; not peer reviewed] ([eye-gaze repo](https://github.com/m-karthika14/eye-gaze)). No peer-reviewed 2024 to 2026 work was found applying a neural noisy-channel decoder to gaze typing, so this is open ground.

Licences are clean for this capability. Typeling and the Supra 25M and 50M base models are Apache-2.0 [read for the Typeling survey; the underlying model cards were not checked] ([Typeling model plan](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling/blob/master/docs/model-plan.md)). llama.cpp is MIT. The traps are in data and in neighbours: DailyDialog is CC BY-NC-SA [extract] ([dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/roskoN/dailydialog)), so the Vertanen group's released AAC model, which used it, likely carries non-commercial provenance [estimate]. FUTO's app is under a source-first licence that is not open source [read, community document]. Meta's MobileLLM line is non-commercial [extract] ([MobileLLM-Pro card](https://huggingface.co/facebook/MobileLLM-Pro/blob/main/README.md)). Liquid's fast LFM2.5 models carry a US$10M revenue cap that passes to every downstream distributor [extract] ([Liquid licence](https://www.liquid.ai/lfm-license)). Typeling's permissive training set is FineWeb, SODA, WildChat-1M, OpenAssistant and Ubuntu IRC, with the caveat it records itself that SODA was generated by an OpenAI model whose terms restrict building competing models [read] ([Typeling README](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling)).

The recommended approach is a two-speed predictor. The n-gram and user cache answer instantly and drive dwell shortening. A 25 to 50M model with a roughly 16K vocabulary, running through llama.cpp on the native side, re-ranks within 20 to 50 ms. Models of 1B and above are reserved for sentence-level work after a pause. Confidence is **medium to high** that this works on Macs and laptops and **medium** on tablets. The headline numbers come from a single project's own benchmarks, read directly but not independently replicated, and no AAC-specific public benchmark exists.

Multilingual support follows the same pattern at higher cost. Every shipped keyboard-sized model is monolingual, with one model of 25 to 42M parameters per language [read and extract] ([futo-basque research notes](https://github.com/itzune/futo-basque/blob/main/RESEARCH.md), [Lexo write-up](https://lexokeyboard.app/writeups/on-device-llm-keyboard/)). English-centric tokenisers split agglutinative words into two to three times more tokens [extract] ([arXiv 2603.20854](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.20854)). No keystroke-savings measurement for Finnish, Turkish or Hungarian was found.

## Sentence replies fit the hardware but not yet the evidence

### What the research supports

The honest calibration number comes from Google's SpeakFaster. In simulation its abbreviation-expansion system reached keystroke-saving rates of 0.64 to 0.66, but the two eye-gaze users with ALS achieved only **0.32 to 0.36**, with text-entry rates **29 to 60%** above their baseline [extract] ([Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53873-3), [arXiv 2312.01532](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01532)). Field observation put ordinary gaze typing at 8.1 words per minute [extract] ([SpeakFaster Observer](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3544549.3573870)). Reading candidates and recovering from misses consume about half the simulated gain. SpeakFaster used a 64B-parameter cloud model, although the precursor paper found that fine-tuned small T5 models beat the few-shot large model in almost all cases, and that one turn of partner context roughly doubled accuracy [extract] ([NAACL 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.naacl-main.91/)).

No published study measures a fully on-device system that listens to the partner and proposes replies to eye-gaze users. The closest shipping product is Lingraphica's Conversations tool, launched in July 2026, which transcribes the partner and suggests replies steered by keywords and reply type; its only evidence is a vendor test with 13 users [extract, vendor claim] ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260708229328/en/Lingraphica-Supports-Spontaneous-Communication-With-New-Conversations-Tool)). Everything else from 2025 and 2026 is qualitative, simulated or tiny.

Two findings shape the design. First, **personal memory beats model size and generic context**. In an MIT-licensed hackathon project with a simulated ALS user, adding personal memory through vector search raised top-1 sentence accuracy from 12.5% to 35.7%, yet a plain word-frequency keyboard still saved more keystrokes (53.0% against 44.8%), and adding time and wearable context made accuracy worse [read; not peer reviewed, synthetic user, cloud model] ([NeuroVoice](https://github.com/mariavarelacaraballo/neuroVoice)). Sentence prediction is therefore a layer on top of a good word predictor, never a replacement. Second, **agency preferences depend on time pressure**. A CHI 2025 study with five AAC users found they would trade agency for speed when timing mattered, such as landing a joke, and rated choosing a "context bubble" from the partner's utterance best [extract] ([arXiv 2410.16634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16634)).

The risks are social more than technical. An AAC-using researcher who trained a personal model on seven months of his own output reported that it resurfaced private details out of context, nudged conversations in directions he did not intend, and that logging changed his behaviour before any AI was involved [extract] ([arXiv 2509.13671](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13671)). Smart replies shift language towards the positive, and people suspected of using them are rated more negatively [extract] ([Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2023/04/study-uncovers-social-cost-using-ai-conversations)).

|---|---|---|

Heat limits sustained use. Over ten minutes unplugged, an iPhone 17 Pro fell from 57.1 to 35.4 tok/s on one runtime and from 45.7 to 25.1 on another [read] ([apple-silicon-llm-bench](https://github.com/john-rocky/apple-silicon-llm-bench)). Prompt processing is the larger latency risk: time to first token for a 1B model rose from 407 ms at 47 prompt tokens to 1,330 ms at 690 [extract, partly inconsistent snippets] ([benchmark mirror](https://hn.jakechampion.name/reader/https://rickytakkar.com/blog_russet_mlx_benchmark.html)). The design answer is a stable cached prompt prefix. llama.cpp's server already offers prompt caching, several completions from one prompt, parallel slots and grammar constraints [read] ([llama.cpp server README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/server/README.md)). A gaze user also has an advantage a voice bot lacks: the partner's turn lasts several seconds, so candidates can be drafted on partial transcripts and be on screen when the turn ends [estimate].

### Why operating-system models cannot be the engine

Apple, Google and Microsoft each expose a built-in model, and each is unsuitable as the only path. Apple's Foundation Models has a **4,096-token** context, no version pinning, and guardrails that check input and output and can refuse even in the permissive mode [read] ([Apple forum 833642](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/833642), [Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/foundationmodels/improving-the-safety-of-generative-model-output)). Android's Gemini Nano applies input and output safety filters [read] ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/ai/gemini-nano)) and is reported as limited to named flagship devices, foreground-only and quota-limited [extract]. Windows Phi Silica needs a Copilot+ PC, package identity and a per-publisher access token, and blocks content above a severity threshold [extract] ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/windows/ai/apis/phi-silica)). A per-publisher token is awkward for an MIT project whose forks could not reuse it [estimate]. Chrome's built-in model is not available to Chromium embedders, which by inference includes Electron [read for CEF; inference for Electron] ([CEF issue 3982](https://github.com/chromiumembedded/cef/issues/3982)).

The filtering point is a values question for this project. AAC users already suffer from censored vocabularies: one study found 73 to 88% of participants had no means to communicate about sexuality topics [extract] ([USSAAC slides](https://isaac-online.org/wp-content/uploads/USSAAC-Webinar-April-27-2022-1.pdf)). A vendor-filtered model reproduces that harm. Open weights under Apache or MIT terms, with user-controlled settings, are the default path; operating-system models are an optional zero-download tier.

For open weights, Gemma 4 moved to Apache 2.0 in April 2026 [extract] ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/gemma-4/)), Qwen3 and Qwen3.5 small models are Apache 2.0 [extract], Granite 4.0 is Apache 2.0 [read] ([Granite repo](https://github.com/ibm-granite/granite-4.0-language-models)), and SmolLM2 and Phi-4-mini are Apache and MIT respectively. Gemma 3 and Llama 3.2 use custom terms, and the EmbeddingGemma retrieval model uses the Gemma licence [extract]. For the memory store, sqlite-vec is dual Apache-2.0 and MIT, pure C, and pre-1.0 [read] ([sqlite-vec](https://github.com/asg017/sqlite-vec)).

The recommended approach is a ladder: automatic candidates spread across intents, then intent chips, then tapping a word in the partner's transcript, then keywords or initials, then ordinary typing. Personalisation starts with a user-editable knowledge file and retrieval over past utterances, since the retrieval evidence is stronger and cheaper than per-user fine-tuning, though the two published comparisons conflict [extract] ([arXiv 2409.09510](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.09510v2), [OPPU](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.372.pdf)). Nothing is spoken without explicit selection. Confidence is **medium** that the compute works on Macs and flagship devices, **low** that it works well on mid-range Android and ordinary Windows laptops, and **low** on how much it helps real users, because no human-rated study of 1 to 4B models on short personal replies exists.

## Partner speech transcribes well, but "who spoke" needs enrolment

### Transcription

Streaming English transcription is a solved engineering problem on every target platform. Three families lead. Moonshine v2 offers streaming models of 34M, 123M and 245M parameters under MIT for both code and weights, with response latencies of 34, 73 and 107 ms on a MacBook Pro and 237, 527 and 802 ms on a Raspberry Pi 5, at 12.0%, 7.84% and 6.65% word error rate on the Open ASR Leaderboard sets [read, from a fork's copy of a benchmark table since removed upstream; latency used a single test file] ([Moonshine fork](https://github.com/thinhlpg/moonshine), [Moonshine](https://github.com/moonshine-ai/moonshine)). NVIDIA's Nemotron Speech Streaming 0.6B was judged the strongest real-time English candidate in a Microsoft study, averaging about 8.2% streaming error across eight benchmarks, shrinking to 0.67 GB at 4-bit, and running more than six times faster than real time on CPU [extract] ([arXiv 2604.14493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14493v2)). On Apple silicon the FluidAudio Core ML port of the same model scored 2.71% on LibriSpeech at 40 times real time [read] ([FluidAudio benchmarks](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FluidInference/FluidAudio/main/Documentation/Benchmarks.md)). Apple's own SpeechAnalyzer scored about 2.1% on LibriSpeech in two benchmarks, both from vendors with a stake [extract] ([Gigazine](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260714-apple-speech-analyzer-benchmark/)).

Clean-speech numbers do not transfer to a living room. The same Parakeet models that score 2.1 to 2.5% on LibriSpeech score **13.5 to 17.2%** on a 60-minute recording [read] ([FluidAudio benchmarks](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FluidInference/FluidAudio/main/Documentation/Benchmarks.md)). The realistic planning figure for far-field household conversation is 10 to 20% word error rate [estimate]. No benchmark of open on-device models on that kind of audio was found. Because the transcript feeds a prediction model and is not a legal record, that error rate is tolerable [estimate]. Atypical speech is a separate problem: every system exceeds 51% error on severe dysarthria [extract] ([Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/96698/)).

### Who spoke

Blind diarisation in real time is cheap in compute and poor in accuracy. On the far-field AMI meeting set, the offline pipeline reaches about 10.6% error, while streaming methods range from **20.7%** for LS-EEND and 26.4% for streaming Sortformer to 39 to 53% for chunked clustering [read] ([FluidAudio benchmarks](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FluidInference/FluidAudio/main/Documentation/Benchmarks.md)). All of those numbers are from Apple silicon; none exists for Android tablets or Windows CPUs.

The tractable formulation is closed-set identification. Family members and carers each enrol a few seconds of speech with consent; every utterance is cut out by voice-activity detection, turned into one speaker embedding and compared with the enrolled profiles; anything below threshold is labelled an unknown speaker [estimate]. The CAM++ embedding shows a mean similarity of 0.805 for the same speaker against 0.256 for different speakers [read; measured on read Mandarin, not household speech] ([FluidAudio benchmarks](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FluidInference/FluidAudio/main/Documentation/Benchmarks.md)). sherpa-onnx provides diarisation, speaker identification and verification on every platform it supports [read] ([sherpa-onnx](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx)). No published accuracy for identifying three to six enrolled voices at a distance was found, and short replies such as "yeah" will be the failure case [estimate].

The app has one advantage over generic voice agents: it knows exactly what its own voice just said. Echo cancellation with the synthesised audio as reference, gating while speech is playing, and text matching against the spoken sentence give three independent ways to exclude the user's own output [estimate, built on standard voice-agent practice] ([Deepgram guide](https://developers.deepgram.com/guides/deep-dives/audio-preprocessing-barge-in)). Turn-end detection is cheap: Pipecat's Smart Turn v3.2 is an 8 MB, BSD-2-Clause model covering 23 languages [read] ([smart-turn](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn)), though one independent evaluation gives it only 58.9% recall [extract] ([AI Engineer talk](https://www.ai.engineer/talks/hMlLw1LeIK8-designing-voice-agents-real-conversations)). That weakness matters little here because predictions refresh on partial transcripts anyway [estimate].

### Device classes, licences and confidence

| Device class | Recommended engine | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Apple-silicon Mac | Parakeet or Nemotron through Core ML, or SpeechAnalyzer; 25 to 150 times real time | [read] for FluidAudio numbers |
| Ordinary Windows laptop | sherpa-onnx on CPU with Moonshine Small or Medium, or Nemotron 4-bit | Moonshine 165 to 269 ms on "Linux x86" [read]; Nemotron above 6 times real time on unnamed CPU [extract]; no NPU numbers |

The cleanest licence fit is Moonshine's English streaming models, which are MIT; its older non-English models are non-commercial [read] ([Moonshine](https://github.com/moonshine-ai/moonshine)). sherpa-onnx and FluidAudio code are Apache-2.0 [read]. Kyutai's speech-to-text weights are CC-BY 4.0 [read] ([Kyutai DSM](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/delayed-streams-modeling)). Nemotron's licence is reported inconsistently as NVIDIA Open Model License or OpenMDW-1.1, and Sortformer is under the NVIDIA Open Model License; both need legal review [extract and read]. The pyannote community-1 diarisation weights are CC-BY-4.0 and gated [extract]. Multilingual streaming is weaker than English across the board.

Recording bystanders is a legal question that on-device processing reduces but does not remove. Roughly 11 to 15 US states require all-party consent [extract; sources disagree and the main chart dates from 2024] ([Kilpatrick Townsend](https://ktslaw.com/en/Insights/Alert/2024/7/Wiretap-Laws-in-the-United-States)), and stored voice embeddings are biometric identifiers under laws such as Illinois BIPA [weak, compliance-vendor source]. Keeping audio in memory only, storing text transiently, showing a visible listening indicator and enrolling voices only with consent are sensible defaults [estimate, not legal advice]. Confidence is **high** for English transcription on Macs and recent Apple devices, **medium** on Windows and Android, **medium** for enrolled-speaker labels and **low** for open diarisation of strangers.

## The user's own voice works from seconds of clean audio

### What works today

The live engine with the best evidence is **Kyutai Pocket TTS**. It has 100M parameters, runs on two CPU cores, produces first audio in about 200 ms, runs about six times faster than real time on a MacBook Air M4, clones a voice from a wav file, covers seven languages and needs no phonemiser [read] ([Pocket TTS README](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/pocket-tts/blob/main/README.md)). A cloned voice exports once to a small voice-state file, so cloning cost is not paid at speak time [read]. Weak laptop CPUs have much less headroom: a four-core cloud x86 machine managed only 2.3 to 2.5 times real time [read]. On Android, a LiteRT port runs at about real time on a Pixel 8a GPU [read], which makes the 100M model marginal on mid-range tablets. Kyutai released English models of 18M, 7M and 2M parameters in October 2026 [read], but whether those tiny models clone voices well could not be confirmed because the blog post was unreachable.

The alternatives each have a catch. NeuTTS Air is Apache 2.0 and clones from three seconds, but needs espeak for phonemes and reaches only 20 tokens per second on a Galaxy A25 against the 50 it needs for real time [read] ([NeuTTS README](https://github.com/neuphonic/neutts/blob/main/README.md)); the smaller NeuTTS Nano has a US$5M revenue cap [read] ([NeuTTS licence](https://github.com/neuphonic/neutts/blob/main/LICENSE)). Chatterbox Nano offers cloning plus laughter and cough tags under MIT code at 110M parameters, but Resemble retired the whole family in August 2026 with no further maintenance [read] ([Chatterbox README](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox/blob/master/README.md)). KittenTTS 2 has cloning, emotion tags and emphasis, but is a 1.7B model needing about 6 GB of RAM on CPU, streams only in whole chunks so short utterances wait for full generation, and its weights carry a separate community licence that could not be read [read] ([KittenTTS README](https://github.com/KittenML/KittenTTS/blob/main/README.md)). Kokoro has Apache weights and no cloning [read and extract]. The most natural open models are either licence-restricted or too heavy: Higgs Audio v3 weights are non-commercial, F5-TTS weights are CC-BY-NC, Fish Audio and IndexTTS use custom licences [read, from each project's README].

No independent listening test could be retrieved. Every quality ranking in the notes is a vendor claim or one of several mutually inconsistent secondary leaderboards, so "sounds human" is unverified for every model named here.

### Cloning from historical audio, with consent

Zero-shot cloning from a cleaned 3 to 30 second reference is the only path certain to run fully on-device on all four device classes [estimate]. The quality of the reference is copied into the voice; Kyutai says so explicitly [read]. Cloning also copies dysarthria: a 2025 study found commercial clones of dysarthric speakers preserved the disorder closely enough to resemble real dysarthric speech [extract] ([arXiv 2503.01266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01266)). An Interspeech 2025 attempt to reconstruct a pre-illness voice with a large open model reported inconsistent intelligibility and speaker identity [extract] ([arXiv 2506.04397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04397v1)). Pre-illness recordings therefore remain the best source, and automatic "voice repair" is still research.

An open pipeline exists for turning messy recordings into usable references. Emilia-Pipe chains music removal, speaker diarisation, segmentation into 3 to 30 second single-speaker clips, transcription and quality scoring, and processes an hour of raw audio in a few minutes [read] ([Emilia-Pipe](https://github.com/open-mmlab/Amphion/blob/main/preprocessors/Emilia/README.md)). Fine-tuning a voice, as opposed to zero-shot cloning, assumes hours of data and an NVIDIA GPU in every repository read; none documents it on a laptop without one [read across repos; the conclusion is an estimate].

Apple Personal Voice is the only operating-system personal voice third-party apps can use. On Apple-Intelligence-class devices it now needs 10 phrases and under a minute [extract] ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/03/19/ios-26-made-one-of-iphones-wildest-most-unique-features-a-lot-better/)). It must be recorded live by someone who can still speak, is reported in only three language variants, and has a list of developer-reported bugs [extract]. Android and Windows have no equivalent. Azure's cloud personal voice forbids pre-existing recordings outright [extract] ([Azure code of conduct](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/legal/cognitive-services/speech-service/tts-code-of-conduct)), which shows that mainstream consent designs assume a speaker who can record today. A project serving people who have already lost speech needs its own consent mechanism: a recorded or documented consent artefact from the person or a legally authorised representative, stored with the voice, with voice files encrypted and never leaving the device [estimate]. No published protocol for this case was found. Open models rely on consent clauses in their READMEs, and none checks consent technically; Pocket TTS prohibits cloning "without explicit and lawful consent" [read].

### Expressiveness, device classes and confidence

AAC users have long reported that they cannot convey emotion, irony or emphasis [extract] ([PMC6802860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6802860)). The combination of own voice, emotion control and a truly small model does not exist off the shelf in permissive form [estimate from the catalogue]. One cheap workaround follows from how cloners behave: because they inherit style from the reference, several voice states per person (neutral, animated, quiet, firm), each built from differently styled historical clips, give the user a "tone" key with no special model. No source validates this idea [estimate].

| Device class | Live cloned voice | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Apple-silicon Mac | Pocket TTS comfortably; larger models up to about 0.6B | About 6 times real time on an M4 [read]; the 2B VoxCPM2 is slower than real time on an M4 Pro [read] |
| Ordinary Windows laptop | Pocket TTS on CPU, expect 2 to 6 times real time; x86 needs AVX2 | [read] for one cloud CPU; no laptop measurement |
| iPad and iPhone | Pocket TTS through Core ML; Personal Voice where the user can still record | FluidAudio ships the port [read]; speed claims are unverified posts [weak] |
| Mid-range Android tablet | Pocket TTS marginal at about real time; smaller variants or the OS voice as fallback | Pixel 8a GPU figure [read]; no tablet measurement |

Pocket TTS code is MIT [read] ([licence](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/pocket-tts/blob/main/LICENSE)); its weights licence was not verified and one secondary site lists it inconsistently as CC BY 4.0 or MIT [weak]. Piper remains GPL-3 with embedded espeak-ng [read]. Confidence is **medium to high** that a cloned voice runs in real time on Macs, laptops and recent Apple devices, **low to medium** on mid-range Android, and **low** on perceived naturalness and on emotional control, since nothing was independently measured.

## The web view draws the keyboard; native code runs the models

The project's Electron and Capacitor shells stay, but models cannot live in the mobile web view. A WebKit bug is titled "Only ~300 MB of RAM is reliably available to WebAssembly pages", Apple confirms the web content memory limit cannot be raised, and the failure mode is a silent page reload [extract for the bug title; read context from Apple forums] ([WebKit bug 269777](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=269777), [Apple forum 766309](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/766309)). A 1B model run through WebGPU reloaded the page mid-generation on an iPhone even though feature detection passed [weak, single report]. WebNN, the only web route to NPUs, is not shipped [extract].

One portable native core covers all four platforms: **llama.cpp** (MIT) for language models and **sherpa-onnx** (Apache-2.0) for voice-activity detection, transcription, speaker identification and speech [read] ([node-llama-cpp](https://github.com/withcatai/node-llama-cpp), [sherpa-onnx](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx)). On desktop both have Node bindings for an Electron utility process. On mobile they sit in one Capacitor native plugin; the existing Capacitor llama.cpp plugin is at version 0.1.x, so writing a thin in-house plugin is the lower-risk choice [estimate]. Accelerators such as MLX and Core ML on Apple, LiteRT-LM on Android GPUs and Foundry Local for Windows NPUs are optional additions. Direct NPU integration per chip is not practical for a small team, and llama.cpp's Android GPU backend explicitly excludes older Adreno 6xx phones [read] ([llama.cpp OpenCL doc](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/docs/backend/OPENCL.md)), so CPU is the planning assumption for mid-range Android.

Memory sets the ceiling more than speed. iOS apps are reported to get about 6 GB even with the increased-memory entitlement [weak, anecdotal], and Android kills on system pressure with no warning API [read] ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/games/optimize/vitals/lmk)). The researchers' tier scheme below is an engineering estimate that needs validation on real devices.

| Tier | Contents | Resident memory | Typical devices |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | n-gram and user cache, OS voice, nothing to download | Under 100 MB | Any device; fallback when hot or low on battery |
| 1 | Voice detection, small streaming transcription, keyboard model, small neural voice | 0.5 to 1 GB | 4 to 6 GB Android, older iPads, 8 GB Windows laptops |
| 2 | Tier 1 plus a 1B-class model loaded on demand | 1.5 to 2.5 GB | 8 GB phones and tablets, 8 GB Macs, 16 GB Windows laptops |
| 3 | Tier 2 plus a 3 to 4B model and heavier cloned voice on demand | 3.5 to 6 GB | 16 GB Macs, M-series iPads, flagship phones |

All four rows are [estimate]. Weights download after install on every platform. Google Play allows model packs up to 1.5 GB each with targeting by RAM and chip [read] ([Play for On-device AI](https://developer.android.com/google/play/on-device-ai)). Apple's position that downloaded weights are data and not code rests on an informal forum answer [extract].

## What a capable coding agent makes possible

This section is brainstorming. Each idea names the open building block it stands on; the idea itself is the researchers' or this report's proposal and none has been validated.

**A purpose-built AAC keyboard model is the highest-value project, and it is cheap.** Typeling measured about 60,000 training tokens per second on a laptop RTX 5070 Ti, so continued pretraining of a 25M model on 150 to 200M tokens takes **40 to 55 minutes** [read] ([Typeling model plan](https://github.com/scarletkc/typeling/blob/master/docs/model-plan.md)). An agent can therefore run dozens of experiments a week on one consumer GPU. The proposal is an Apache-2.0 base of 25 to 50M parameters, a 16K vocabulary, permissive chat corpora, and synthetic AAC-style dialogue written by an Apache-licensed teacher such as Gemma 4 or Qwen3.5, which avoids the terms-of-use ambiguity attached to data generated by closed models. Google found that synthetic data resembling real typing improved small on-device models by 19 to 23% relative, with a remaining gap to real user data [extract] ([arXiv 2404.04360](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.04360)). Per-language models then cost under an hour each.

**A custom decoder that takes gaze uncertainty as input.** Combining FUTO-style soft keystroke tokens with the Vertanen search gives a model that reads a probability over keys and returns calibrated next-letter probabilities. Sharing the model's cache across beam hypotheses in llama.cpp, instead of copying it as Typeling's PyTorch prototype does, should cut the 25 ms query time substantially [read for the bottleneck; the gain is an estimate]. The same decoder can enforce abbreviation expansion as a hard constraint, where each word must start with a typed initial. Published abbreviation results use a 64B model, so whether a small model reaches useful accuracy is unknown; fine-tuned small T5 models beating the large one is the encouraging precedent [extract].

**An evaluation harness built before any model.** Typeling's design simulates typing each word letter by letter and reports keystroke savings, top-three accuracy, latency percentiles and a mistyped-input variant [read]. Extensions worth building are simulated gaze noise at several levels, a time model that charges for reading candidates and for each dwell (the reason simulation overstates real gains by half), per-device runs on all four classes, and held-out tests on the user's own messages split by date. Large-model judges agree poorly with humans on social dialogue [extract] ([ICLR 2026](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10014636)), so any automated judge needs calibration against human labels. The harness would also produce the measurements this research could not find anywhere: small-model latency on mid-range Android, ordinary Windows CPUs and inside web views.

**Per-user adaptation in four steps of rising cost.** The count-based user cache comes first. Retrieval over a user-editable knowledge file and past utterances comes second. An optional overnight LoRA on the user's own Mac or PC comes third, with mlx-lm supporting LoRA and QLoRA on Apple silicon [read] ([mlx-lm](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-lm/blob/main/mlx_lm/LORA.md)); style should come from the adapter and facts only from retrieval, so facts stay editable and deletable [estimate]. Hypernetworks that emit a per-user adapter in one forward pass, with no training on the device, are the research-stage fourth step [extract] ([arXiv 2609.24979](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24979)). For a user who can no longer produce much text, an import pipeline over sent messages, email and banked phrases, expanded with partner prompts written by a local model, is plausible but unstudied.

**A distilled reply suggester.** A 300M to 1B model trained on synthetic triplets of context, steering input and reply, with intent tags so four candidates differ by intent and not by sampling luck, follows SpeakFaster's recipe of about 1.8M synthetic triplets [extract]. A small local re-ranker trained on which suggestions the user picks, edits or rejects has precedent in a Google patent [extract] ([US10846618B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10846618B2/en)) and no evidence in AAC.

**Speech engineering around the household.** Contextual biasing with contact names and the user's vocabulary works today for transducer models in sherpa-onnx with no retraining [extract] ([sherpa-onnx hotwords](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/hotwords/index.html)). Smart Turn publishes its training script and data, so the turn detector can be retrained on AAC-paced conversation [read]. Distilling a large offline recogniser into a small streaming one is a research-scale effort with no open recipe.

**A voice studio.** A guided import tool built on Emilia-Pipe can find the best clean clips in old videos and voicemails, build several styled voice states, and record the consent artefact. On a capable machine, a larger permissive model such as Qwen3-TTS or VoxCPM2 (both Apache-2.0 per their READMEs) can pre-render the user's frequent phrases in several styles for instant playback, with real banked recordings taking priority in the same cache [estimate]. Synthesising the committed part of a message in the background makes "Speak" feel instant. Distilling an expressive large model into a small one for a single voice has no supporting evidence and conflicts with one team's warning that synthetic training data degrades voice fine-tunes [read] ([Orpheus README](https://github.com/canopyai/Orpheus-TTS/blob/main/README.md)).

What an agent cannot do sets the real schedule. It cannot measure anything on a physical iPad, Android tablet or eye tracker, judge whether a voice sounds like the person, run sessions with AAC users, or settle the consent and recording law. Those need people and hardware.

## What changes in the v1 plan

The v1 plan's shape survives. Operating-system voices and an n-gram predictor with a user-adaptive layer remain the right tier-0 default on every device, AI stays optional, and the plan's separation of context providers from prediction providers matches how partner transcripts and personal memory should feed prediction. Message banking, Personal Voice as a later priority, and keeping GPL espeak-ng out of the core are all confirmed.

Five points change. First, the plan states that on-device language models manage 6 to 18 tokens per second on mid-range phones and are too slow to be the default. That holds for general 1B-and-larger models but not for a keyboard-sized model, which decodes at 2,800 tokens per second on a laptop CPU [read] and has not yet been measured on a phone or tablet; a neural predictor is a realistic early addition, not a distant plugin, once it is measured on tablets. Second, the plan's prediction interface should treat the n-gram as the instant first answer and let a neural provider replace it asynchronously, and should carry soft key probabilities from the gaze pipeline into the predictor. Third, the plugin model needs a host-level inference service: models must run in native code behind the host bridge, because a sandboxed JavaScript plugin inside a mobile web view cannot hold them. Fourth, the plan's voice notes need correcting. Supertonic's repository is now an archive with no cloning pipeline [read] ([Supertonic README](https://github.com/supertone-inc/supertonic/blob/main/README.md)), and the plan's statement that Pocket TTS weights are CC-BY is unverified. Fifth, Tatoeba as a corpus candidate is joined by a tested permissive set (FineWeb, WildChat, OpenAssistant, Ubuntu IRC), and the plan's choice of plain files over SQLite will need revisiting when personal-memory retrieval arrives, since that wants a vector index.

## Conclusion

The pattern across all three capabilities is that model size is the wrong axis to plan around. A 25M keyboard model, a 34 to 245M recogniser, a 100M voice and a few megabytes of speaker embedding do most of the work, and they fit the weakest target devices. The large general model is the optional top layer, useful as a teacher during training and as a sentence engine on capable hardware. That inverts the usual assumption that on-device AI means waiting for phones to run bigger models, and it means the project's weakest device classes are served by the same design as its strongest.

The binding constraints are now evidence and consent, not compute. Nobody has published what a mid-range tablet actually does with these models, whether listeners find the small cloned voices human, or how much reply suggestion helps a person typing by gaze outside a two-person study. A project that builds the evaluation harness first, measures on real devices, and designs the consent and privacy flows for people who can no longer speak would be producing knowledge the field lacks, as well as a keyboard.
